## 1. How it was studied

- Read: `RULES.md` (rules 20 and the history of rules 4 to 19), the game brief, the creative bible, the earlier design review (`docs/studio/02`), and the owner's past decisions on events, Market Days and Closing Day (DEC-029, DEC-079, DEC-115, DEC-120).
- Measured: a new read-only script, `docs/studio/review-21/arc.ts`, played 1,200 all-computer games on the shipped engine (300 each at 3 stalls 10 days, 4 stalls 10 days, 3 stalls 14 days and 2 stalls 10 days, Hard level) and counted what happens on each day. Results: `docs/studio/review-21/arc-results.jsonl`.
- Also ran `npm run balance` (150 games a test).
- **Limits.** These are computer games, not people. There is still no playtest evidence (the brief's open questions stand). Computers do not get bored, so "it feels repetitive" is the owner's observation; the numbers below show why it would.

## 2. What the numbers say

Figures are for 3 stalls, 10 days, Hard computers, per stall, unless said otherwise.

### 2.1 Money stops being a decision after Day 4

| Day | Cash at dawn | Cash left after buying | Stalls with $10 or more left over |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $11 | $2 | 5% |
| 3 | $16 | $8 | 36% |
| 5 | $27 | $19 | 66% |
| 7 | $49 | $40 | 90% |
| 10 | $84 | $80 | 99% |

On Days 1 to 3 a stall cannot afford everything it wants, so every dollar is a choice. From Day 5 on, a stall can always afford its 4 pieces, and there is nothing else to spend on: an upgrade costs $3 to $6 and a stall builds **1.0** in a whole 10-day game. In a 14-day game stalls finish with $142 they never had a use for.

A stall that **never builds an upgrade** ends about $4 behind in a $98 game, and no upgrade order ("rush the Juice Bar", "rush the Handcart") wins more than another within the noise of the run. Computers almost never build the Handcart (0 to 0.03 per stall) or the Premium Display (0.01 to 0.1). So the one "build" decision in the game barely moves the result.

### 2.2 Every day from Day 4 is the same day

| Day | Pieces bought | Sales that day | Share of demand served |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.9 | $11.5 | 56% |
| 4 | 3.0 | $16.8 | 63% |
| 6 | 3.2 | $16.4 | 64% |
| 7 | 3.1 | $16.9 | 65% |
| 9 | 3.1 | $18.8 | 74% |
| 10 | 2.0 | $18.5 | 79% |

Buying is about 3 pieces a day all game. Sales sit at $16 to $19 a day from Day 4 to the end; the only bumps are Days 5 and 8, when a standing order is collected ($23 to $24). In the 14-day game, Days 6 to 13 are alike on every measure, and stalls hold 1.8 of their 2 upgrades by Day 7, so the last seven days bring no new kind of decision at all.

**The Standard game's last Market Day is empty.** Day 9 of a 10-day game brings no standing order (it could not be collected), and its upgrade would work for one day and is worth nothing at the end: stalls hold 1.0 upgrades on Day 9 and 1.0 on Day 10. The last day itself is an ordinary day (rules 20, the owner's choice).

### 2.3 You can only plan one day ahead

What a player can see coming: tomorrow's regular, the backs of tomorrow's walk-ins, tomorrow's lorry, and the fixed Market Days. Events are a surprise each morning (rules 13). Yet the fruit was built for longer plans: apples keep 6 days, mangoes need 2 nights. With nothing known beyond tomorrow, there is nothing to aim that at.

### 2.4 Few things to do besides buy, stock and price

| In a 10-day game, per stall | How often |
|---|---|
| Favour cards earned | 1.0 |
| Favour cards played | 0.8 |
| Upgrades built | 1.0 |
| Standing orders at the table | 1.8, each won by one stall |

A player meets a favour card about once a game. The 12-card favour deck, six kinds of card with their own pictures, is nearly unused.

### 2.5 The secret price is often not a contest

- Only **27% to 47%** of customer orders have two or more stalls offering that fruit (3 stalls); the rest have one seller.
- **73% to 87%** of pieces sell within $1 of the customer's top price (`npm run balance`, 4 stalls and 2 stalls).
- A third of what customers want goes unserved, and about **1.2** pieces spoil per stall in a whole game.

Fruit is short, so most of the time the right price is simply the most the customer pays. The game's two pillars, "outprice rivals in secret" and "race against spoilage", bite less often than the screen suggests.

### 2.6 What already works (keep)

- **The race stays open.** The Day 5 leader wins 54% to 56% of 3-stall games (33% would be pure chance), with 3.2 lead changes a game. Poorest-buys-first does its job.
- **Days 1 to 3 are the best part of the game**: cash is tight, so buying is a real choice.
- **The standing order** is the one true multi-day plan: bid, prepare, deliver.
- No fruit is the "right" fruit: profit per piece bought is $2.4 to $3.9 for all four.

## 3. Diagnosis in one paragraph

The game has a strong **day** but no **season**. A strategy game asks the player to give something up now for something later, and to aim at a goal several turns away. Here the only scarce thing, cash, stops being scarce on Day 5; the only things to aim at are tomorrow's customers and one standing order; and the days do not change in size or kind. So a 14-day game is the Day 4 decision made ten more times.

## 4. Design rules for any change

1. **Reuse before adding.** The game already has 20 events, 20 lorries, 36 customers, 6 favours, 5 upgrades and 8 orders. New strategy should come from how they are dealt and seen, not from new card types.
2. **No longer day.** A change may add at most one quick choice a day (the evening already exists as a shared step, DEC-115).
3. **Playable on a table**, with cards and counters (the print kit).
4. **Respect past decisions**: no Closing Day or jam maker (DEC-120); the poorest stall buys first; secret stalls.
5. **Measured before shipping**: each proposal names the number it must move.

## 5. Proposals

Each stands alone. "Goal 1" is planning and actions; "Goal 2" is a varied early, middle and end.

### A. The notice board: two big days known from the start (Goal 1)

- **What.** At set-up, two event cards (three in a Long game) are turned face up and pinned to fixed days, one in the middle of the game and one near the end. Everyone sees them from Day 1 on the day strip. Every other morning still turns over a surprise event, as now.
- **Why.** It gives the fruit's lives a purpose. A Mango Craze on Day 7 means buying Green mangoes on Day 5. A Bake-Off means holding apples. A Heatwave means clearing the shelf the day before. A Health Inspector means selling Soft fruit first.
- **Reuses** the 20 event cards. One new sentence in the rules.
- **Care.** It partly undoes rules 13 (events not shown early), for two days only.
- **Must move:** stalls holding fruit for more than one night before a known event (computers must be taught to plan for it), and the owner's own play.
- **Size:** small to medium.

### B. Three seasons: the decks are dealt in three parts (Goal 2)

- **What.** The customer deck and the lorry deck are each built in three stacks, as card games with "ages" do:
  - **Opening days** (first third): small lorries, small orders, more Bargain customers. Cash is tight. As now, the best part.
  - **High season** (middle): today's game. Standing orders, upgrades, the first known big day.
  - **Fair week** (last third): the big lorries (Convoy, Overstock), the big orders, more Premium customers and the guests. More fruit on every stall means more orders with two sellers, so the secret price matters most at the end, and old fruit is a real danger.
- **Why.** The days grow: takings might run $11, then $17, then $25 a day. Late days are worth more, so the game stays open to the end, and each third asks a different question: *afford it*, then *plan it*, then *win the price fight*.
- **No new rule for the player**: "the market gets busier as the season goes on". On a table, each card carries a mark I, II or III.
- **Care.** Rules 4 added late customers without late fruit and emptied the market (DEC-029). Lorries and customers must grow together.
- **Must move:** sales per day rising across the thirds; orders with two or more sellers above 50% in the last third; fruit spoiled 2 to 3 per stall; the Day 5 leader still winning no more than about 55%.
- **Size:** medium, mostly balance runs.

### C. The town fair: three ribbons to win at the end (Goals 1 and 2)

- **What.** At set-up three ribbon cards are dealt face up from a small deck. At the end each pays cash to the stall that did the most of something, with half for second place. Examples: most blueberries sold; most Soft and Okay pieces sold; most customers served all by yourself; most Premium customers served; most pieces delivered to standing orders; least fruit spoiled.
- **Why.** It is something to aim at from the first day, it differs every game, it rewards choosing a kind of stall to be, and it gives the last days a race and the results a prize-giving, with no special last-day rule.
- **Care.** Ribbons must not simply go to whoever is already richest: pick things that do not follow cash, and pay second place. The count needs a place on screen and on the stall sheet (which already counts fruit sold).
- **Must move:** ribbons change the winner in about 10% to 20% of games (more would feel random, fewer pointless).
- **Size:** medium.

### D. Money that works: favour cards for sale each evening (Goal 1)

- **What.** Each evening one favour card lies face up. Every stall secretly names a price, or passes; the highest pays and takes it (a tie goes to the poorer stall). It happens inside the evening step that already exists.
- **Why.** It gives cash a use for the whole game, and the price sets itself: cheap while everyone is poor, dear when cash is plentiful. It brings the six favour cards into play (today about one a game), so players have more actions: buying first, a free piece, a peek, a fridge. It is a second secret price, which is the game's own idea.
- **Care.** One more quick choice a day. The richest stall may win most cards, but every dollar spent is a dollar of score, and serving a customer alone still earns a card free.
- **Must move:** favour cards played per stall from 0.8 to about 3 or 4 a game; cash left idle on the last day well down; game length up by no more than a minute.
- **Size:** medium (the bid screen exists for standing orders).

### E. Fix the empty Market Day and the weak upgrades (Goals 1 and 2)

- **What.** Small repairs to what is there:
  - Day 9 of a Standard game gets a purpose (with C: "the ribbons are counted from today at double", or simply no Market Day there).
  - Upgrades can be built on **any** evening from Day 1, one an evening, still two to a stall. On Days 1 to 3 cash is tight, so "fruit today or an upgrade for the rest of the game" becomes the opening choice.
  - Replace or rework the two upgrades computers never build (Handcart, Premium Display).
- **Size:** small.

### F. Reserve from tomorrow's lorry (Goal 1) — optional, later

- **What.** In the evening, pay $1 to set aside one piece from tomorrow's lorry (two at most). It is yours at dawn at its usual cost.
- **Why.** Tomorrow's lorry is already shown; this lets a player act on it.
- **Care.** It works against poorest-buys-first, which keeps games close. Only worth trying after B, when fruit is less scarce.

## 6. What I would do, in order

| Step | Package | Why this order |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A + E**: the notice board, upgrades any evening, the empty Day 9 | Smallest. Adds planning at once and an opening choice. Little balance risk |
| 2 | **B**: three seasons | The direct answer to "every day feels the same". Needs the most measuring, so it gets its own package |
| 3 | **C**: the town fair | Best after B, so the ribbons race happens in a busy Fair week |
| 4 | **D**: favours for sale | Adds an action a day; easiest to judge once the season has a shape |

Each step would be built first as a **trial option** on the preview copy (like the "Basic game" and "Fixed prices" options in `RULES.md`), measured with computer games, played by the owner, and only then made the standard rules (rules version 21, which starts new Daily boards and ends games saved halfway, as every rules change does).

## 7. Risks and costs

- **Learning load.** The earlier review already worried the game has many systems for a new player. A and B add almost nothing to learn; C and D each add one idea. The How to play pages and the League's teaching stages would need a page each.
- **Computer players** must be taught each change (plan for a known event, value a ribbon, bid for a favour), or solo games get easier.
- **Table edition.** B needs marks on the customer and lorry cards; C needs ribbon cards and a count; D needs a way to bid in secret (the standing order already does).
- **The Daily market, Sprint and League** run on the same rules; League stages are fixed seeds and would be re-checked.
- **Money cost:** RM0. No new service or dependency.
- **Not proven by play.** Everything here rests on computer games and design reasoning. A handful of real games before and after each step would tell more than any table above.

2. Is it acceptable that two events a game are known in advance (A), given rules 13 made events a morning surprise?
